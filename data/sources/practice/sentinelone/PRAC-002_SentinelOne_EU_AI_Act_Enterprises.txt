Introduction to the EU AI Act
In March 2024, the European Parliament approved the Artificial Intelligence Act, widely regarded as the
world’s first comprehensive AI regulatory framework of this scale. The final Regulation entered into force on
August 1, 2024, with obligations applying on a phased timeline starting the compliance clock for many organi-
zations developing, deploying, or making AI systems available in the EU. Several key milestones have already
passed. The EU AI Act’s ban on certain prohibited AI practices took effect in February 2025. New obligations
for providers of General-Purpose AI (GPAI) models became applicable in August 2025. The majority of the EU
AI Act’s obligations, including transparency requirements and requirements for many high-risk AI systems,
apply from August 2026.
Note: The EU AI Act implementation timeline is currently subject to potential changes as part of the EU’s
Digital Omnibus simplification process. Unless and until changes are formally adopted, organizations should
prepare against the current applicable deadlines and monitor for updates.
The EU AI Act aims to address risks, promote trustworthy and responsible AI, and establish standards for AI
applications. It sets out to promote safe and lawful AI while protecting health, safety, and fundamental rights,
all the while encouraging innovation and adoption of AI systems.
The EU AI Act defines an AI system as a machine-based system designed to operate with varying levels of au-
tonomy, that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that infers, from input, how to generate outputs
such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environ-
ments. This definition is the harmonized standard across all EU member states.
A threshold step for any EU AI Act assessment is determining the organization’s role. An enterprise may
be a deployer for some AI systems, a provider for others, and may take on additional obligations if it places
an AI system on the EU market under its own name or trademark, substantially modifies an AI system, or
changes its intended purpose. Role allocation should therefore be assessed on a system-by-system and
use-case-by-use-case basis.
GenAI systems are not automatically high-risk solely because they use generative AI; however, the way they
are developed, marketed, deployed, or used can make them high-risk. How GenAI systems are categorized in
terms of risk depends on how they are developed, provided, deployed, or repurposed. GenAI is a technology
description, not a risk category under the Act. A single GenAI system may span multiple risk tiers depending
on its use case.
To deploy GenAI responsibly and reduce compliance risk, organizations need a clear picture of what the Act
demands and where they stand today.
This document is provided for general informational purposes only and does not constitute legal advice. It is intended to provide
a high-level overview of selected EU AI Act considerations and is not exhaustive. The EU AI Act is complex and evolving, and
organizations should consult their legal advisors to assess their specific obligations based on their role, AI systems, use cases,
deployment context, and applicable regulatory guidance.

How Does the EU AI Act Apply
to GenAI Systems?
The EU AI Act is often explained through four risk tiers for AI systems placed on the market or put into service
in the EU. Before diving into the four tiers, organizations should remember that some EU AI Act obligations
apply regardless of risk category. For example, providers and deployers must ensure sufficient AI literacy
among relevant staff.
Here are the four tiers:
1. Minimal Risk
Minimal-risk systems carry no additional risk-category mandatory obligations under the EU AI Act,
though voluntary codes of conduct for responsible use are encouraged.
2. Limited Risk / Transparency Obligations
AI systems commonly described as “limited risk” are generally those whose main EU AI Act obligations
are transparency obligations.
Transparency obligations apply from August 2, 2026. These obligations sit outside the tier classification
and apply across AI system types based on specific characteristics or use cases. They cover AI systems
intended to interact directly with humans, systems that generate synthetic content, emotion recognition
or biometric categorization systems, deepfakes, and certain AI-generated text disclosures. For example,
an AI-powered chatbot on a customer service website may include a disclaimer reading “You are chatting
with an AI assistant.”
3. High Risk
In addition to transparency obligations where applicable, high-risk systems are subject to extensive re-
quirements. A system may be high-risk because it is part of a regulated product, such as certain medical
devices or machinery, or because it is used in a sensitive area listed in the EU AI Act, such as employment,
education, access to essential services, law enforcement, migration, or administration of justice.
4. Unacceptable Risk
AI practices presenting unacceptable risk are prohibited under the EU AI Act. Prohibited practices include:
• use harmful manipulation or exploit vulnerabilities in ways that materially distort behavior and cause,
or are likely to cause, significant harm;
• conduct prohibited social scoring;
• assess criminal-offense risk based solely on profiling or personality traits, subject to the EU AI Act’s
exception for systems supporting human assessments based on objective facts;
• create or expand facial-recognition databases through untargeted scraping from the internet or
CCTV footage;
• infer emotions in workplace or education settings, except for medical or safety reasons;
• use biometric categorization to infer protected characteristics; or
• use real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement, except in the
limited cases permitted by the EU AI Act.

These prohibitions have been in full effect since February 2, 2025. AI practices that fall within these prohibited
categories may trigger the EU AI Act’s highest tier of administrative fines.
General-Purpose AI (GPAI)
GPAI and GenAI are not the same thing. A GPAI model is a model trained on large amounts of data that dis-
plays significant generality and is capable of competently performing a wide range of distinct tasks. Many, but
not all, GenAI systems rely on GPAI models. Since August 2, 2025, all providers of GPAI must supply technical
documentation and information for downstream AI system providers, implement a policy to comply with EU
copyright law, publish a summary of training content... GPAI systems that present systemic risk face addi-
tional obligations, including model evaluations, adversarial testing, serious incident tracking, and adequate
cybersecurity protections.
The Cross-Cutting Requirement for AI Literacy
Beyond enforcement mechanisms, the EU AI Act also establishes a key cross-cutting obligation for providers
and deployers of AI systems that applies regardless of risk tier.
While much of the EU AI Act focuses on specific risk-based obligations, Article 4 introduces a broad require-
ment that applies to all providers and deployers of AI systems as of February 2, 2025. All AI providers and
deployers are legally obligated to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among their staff and other persons
dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on their behalf.
This means that compliance is not just an IT or Legal problem. It is also an HR and operational issue. Enterpris-
es must actively train relevant employees to understand the AI tools they use, how they work, the limitations
of those tools, and the risks they pose regarding privacy, security, and bias.

Incident Response Preparedness
A significant portion of the EU AI Act focuses on defining high-risk AI systems and delineating the require-
ments to which such systems are subject.
Article 6 of the EU AI Act lays out the criteria that categorize AI systems as high-risk.
• Article 6(1) covers AI systems linked to certain regulated products, such as medical devices, machinery,
toys, or lifts, where those products require third-party conformity assessment.
• Article 6(2) specifies categories of high-risk systems, including areas with high potential for impact
on humans, by referring to the use cases listed in Annex III to the EU AI Act (e.g. biometrics, safety
components in the management and operation of critical digital infrastructure, etc.) which can be
amended by the European Commission.
Depending on the system and the organization’s role, high risk systems may entail the
following obligations:
Maintain a risk management system and post-market monitoring processes to identify,
evaluate, and address relevant risks.
Prepare and maintain required technical documentation, instructions for use, and
automatic logging capabilities.
Register certain high-risk AI systems in the EU database, where required.
Enable effective human oversight, including measures that allow appropriate human
intervention where required.
Carry out required risk management measures and, where applicable, fundamental
rights impact assessments before deployment.
Apply required data governance and data management practices, including for training,
validation, and testing data where applicable.
Complete the applicable conformity assessment procedure before placing the system on
the market or putting it into service.
Report serious incidents to the competent authorities where required.
Note: The EU AI Act implementation timeline is currently subject to potential changes. A provisional agree-
ment has been reached that would adjust the timeline for applying rules on high-risk AI systems, but has
not yet been formally adopted. Unless and until changes are formally adopted, organizations should prepare
against the current applicable deadlines and monitor for updates.

Conditions for Gaining an Exemption
From High-Risk Classification
In addition to specifying categories of high-risk systems, article 6(3) sets out conditions under which certain
Annex III systems that otherwise would be considered high-risk can become exempt from such categorization.
The primary requirement for exemption is not posing significant risk of harm to individuals’ health, safety and
fundamental rights, including by not materially influencing the outcome of decision making.
To be eligible, a system must not profile humans (doing so automatically disqualifies systems from exemption)
and must be intended to do one of the following:
• Perform a narrow procedural task.
• Improve the result of a previously completed human activity.
• Detect decision-making patterns or deviations from prior decision-making patterns, without replacing or
influencing a previously completed human assessment without proper human review.
• Perform a preparatory task to an assessment relevant for the purposes of the use cases identified as
high-risk under Annex III.
Providers relying on this exemption must document their assessment before the system is placed on the mar-
ket or put into service and provide that documentation to competent authorities upon request.
Where applicable, providers must register themselves and their AI system in the EU Database for High-Risk
AI Systems. Providers who have concluded that their system is not high-risk under Article 6(3) may also be
required to register themselves and the system in the database before placing it on the market or putting it
into service.

Data Governance and Security
Understanding who is responsible for meeting the EU AI Act’s data governance and security requirements can
be challenging as obligations depend on the AI system, use case, and the organization’s role. From the array
of misconceptions surrounding these requirements, we have chosen to address three of the most common:
MISCONCEPTION #1
“EU AI Act responsibilities are the same for SaaS and on-premise.”
The EU AI Act does not allocate obligations based on delivery model alone, but rather applies based on the or-
ganization’s role, the AI system, and the relevant use case. Compliance responsibility differs based on whether
an organization acts as an importer, a distributor, or in another role defined under the EU AI Act. Understand-
ing which role applies to your organization is essential for determining your obligations.
In the case of SaaS deployments, “deployers” – organizations or individuals that use an AI system in a profes-
sional capacity – access tools via the internet, with third-party “providers” – organizations or individuals that
develop the AI system or GPAI model and place it on the market or put it into service under their own name or
trademark – supplying the necessary software and hardware. The applicable obligations for each party will
depend on the AI system’s risk classification, use case, and each organization’s role under the Act.
Conversely, with on-premise AI systems, organizations may have greater control over the system, infrastructure,
configuration, and data flows . While this model may increase the organization’s operational responsibilities and
affect its role under the EU AI Act, it also offers greater control and customization. With greater oversight and
access, the organization can more directly manage certain controls relevant to its EU AI Act obligations.
MISCONCEPTION #2
“Non-EU organizations are not subject to the EU AI Act’s requirements.”
The EU AI Act applies to AI systems placed on the EU market, put into service in the EU, or used in a way that
falls within the Act’s territorial scope, including certain cases where the outputs of an AI system are used in
the EU. This is without regard for where companies are based.
MISCONCEPTION #3
“These requirements may soon become outdated, as the EU AI Act will not
retain global credibility.”
The EU’s strong regulatory influence, coupled with its comprehensive approach to managing emerging tech-
nologies, positions the EU AI Act as a potential cornerstone for AI regulation worldwide. Like how the General
Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) set a global standard for data protection, the EU AI Act could serve as a
key reference point for AI governance across different jurisdictions. Just as GDPR compelled most companies
worldwide to align with European standards, the EU AI Act may influence the development of global AI policies
and drive greater regulatory convergence.

Penalties and Enforcement
Mechanisms
Violations of the EU AI Act’s provisions may result in the following penalties:
• Companies that violate the EU AI Act’s prohibited AI practices rules face fines of up to €35 million or up
to 7% of their total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year.
• Failure to comply with certain other requirements set by the EU AI Act (e.g. transparency obligations)
will result in fines of up to €15 million or up to 3% of total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding
financial year.
• Supplying notified bodies or national competent authorities with incorrect, incomplete or misleading
information in reply to a request will result in fines of up to €7.5 million or up to 1% of total worldwide
annual turnover for the preceding financial year.
For SMEs (including startups), the fine will be the lower of the two amounts (between the maximum percentage
and the maximum fixed amount). For all other entities, the fine will be the higher of the two.
The EU AI Act includes several mechanisms for enforcing compliance with its requirements:
1. National competent authorities (NCAs)
Each EU member state designates an NCA (or multiple NCAs) to oversee enforcement of the EU AI Act.
NCAs are responsible for market surveillance and enforcement within their jurisdiction and have the author-
ity to conduct inspections, audits and investigations as needed.
2. The European AI Office and AI Board
The European AI Office is now active and has supported the development of the GPAI Code of Practice
published by the European Commission. National Competent Authorities in each member state are now
responsible for market surveillance and enforcement of the February 2025 prohibitions.
3. Market Surveillance
The EU AI Act allows national authorities to conduct inspections, checks and audits of AI systems. Where
required, it also allows designated conformity assessment bodies to assess whether or not AI systems
conform with the EU AI Act.

Preparing AI Systems for Compliance
To align with the EU AI Act’s requirements, organizations’ security and risk management leaders shouldcatalog
and analyze AI-enabled use cases so as to better understand these use cases’ defining characteristics. This will
help them assess the risk of their AI-enabled use cases and identify applicable roles, risk categories, and obliga-
tions under the EU AI Act.
The EU AI Act may impose obligations on organizations that deploy AI capabilities embedded in third-party
tools, so it is especially crucial that organizations understand third-party AI risks. Sourcing, procurement, and
vendor management representatives should work with vendors to understand where AI is used in third-party
products and services, and multidisciplinary teams should be formed to address related contractual commit-
ments, data flows, and operational controls. Standardized vendor questionnaires and vendor attestations can
help organizations review lower-risk use cases.
